# AI Writing Patterns — the full detection list

Numbered strongest first. Patterns 1–5 justify an edit on a single sighting.
Patterns marked *(weak alone)* count only when several tells share a passage,
because a careful writer may use any one of them on purpose.

## A. Staging instead of stating

### 1. Not X but Y
**Watch for:** "not just X, but Y"; "not merely X, it's Y"; "it's not X, it's
Y"; "X, rather than Y"; the same contrast split across two sentences ("This
does not mean X. It means Y."); the clipped negative tail ("..., no guessing").
**Why it is a tell:** the negative half answers an objection nobody made, so
the positive half sounds bigger than it is.
**Before:** "It's not just a song; it's a statement."
**After:** "The song works as a statement."
**Before (split):** "This does not mean every choice is equal. It means no
external system confirms the right choice."
**After:** "No external system confirms the right choice, although the
choices still carry different consequences."

### 2. One-line closers and dramatic fragments
**Watch for:** a one-sentence paragraph that restates the previous one;
"That is the real win."; "Let that sink in."; "Read that again."; the same
closer after several sections; a sentence after an example that names what
it showed ("This shows the importance of..."); fragment rows ("No prior. No
nostalgia."); ALL CAPS or periods-between-words emphasis.
**Before:** "Then the model arrived. No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia. The
old rules were gone."
**After:** "The model arrived without preferences trained into it, which
made some older assumptions less useful."

### 3. Sayings that sound deep
**Watch for:** "the real question is", "at its core", "what really matters",
"fundamentally", "the heart of the matter", "X is the Y of Z", "X becomes a
trap", "the language of", "the currency of".
**Before:** "At its core, what really matters is organizational readiness."
**After:** "Whether the change sticks depends mostly on whether the
organization is ready to change its habits."

### 4. Staged run-up before the point
**Watch for:** "Let's dive in", "Here's what you need to know", "Honestly?",
"Look,", "Here's the thing", "Let's be honest", "Quick note:", "heads up".
**Before:** "Is it worth the price? Honestly? It depends on usage."
**After:** "Whether it is worth the price depends on how often it gets used."

### 5. Arguing with no one
**Watch for:** "This isn't mainly about", "I'm not saying", "To be clear",
"Don't get me wrong", "This is not to say", "Some might say... but", "A
tempting approach would be", "One might be tempted to".
**Before:** "This isn't mainly about prompt length, and I'm not arguing
documentation doesn't matter. The issue is whether the agent can use the
instruction when it acts."
**After:** "The issue is whether the agent can use the instruction when it
acts."

## B. Rhythm by rule

### 6. Forced triads
Ideas arrive in threes to sound complete. Check that each item adds a
distinct idea; merge, develop the strongest, or vary the structure when they
do not. Keep three real items when the meaning needs three.
**Before:** "Attendees can expect innovation, inspiration, and industry
insights."
**After:** "Attendees get practical workshops and time to talk between
sessions."

### 7. Repeated sentence openings
Several sentences in a row start with the same subject. Merge, change the
subject, or begin with the action. A deliberate repetition ("She came. She
saw. She conquered.") can stay.
**Before:** "She noted the door. She noted the lock on it. She filed both
away."
**After:** "She noted the door and its lock, then filed both away."

### 8. Dashes as the universal connector *(weak alone)*
One dash is an editor's habit; a page full of them is a model's. Replace
with period, comma, colon, or parentheses. Keep dashes inside code, paths,
URLs, and quoted material.

### 9. Stacked qualifiers *(weak alone)*
"could potentially possibly be argued", "it may in some cases perhaps".
Keep qualifiers the source supports. Ordinary single hedges ("perhaps",
"tends to") are human and stay.

### 10. Hyphenated pairs everywhere *(weak alone)*
Keep the hyphen before a noun ("a high-quality report"); drop it after
("the report is high quality"). Dictionary words like "third-party" keep
theirs everywhere.

### 11. Passive voice and missing subjects *(weak alone)*
"No configuration file needed. Results are preserved automatically." Name
the actor when it clarifies: "You do not need a configuration file. The
system preserves results automatically." Academic writing legitimately uses
some passive; fix the agentless ones that hide responsibility.

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

### 12. Overused AI words
delve, testament, landscape (abstract), showcase, tapestry, pivotal, robust
(figurative), meticulous, intricacies, interplay, garner, bolster, enhance,
crucial, key (adjective), enduring, vibrant, valuable, underscore, align
with, deep dive, gate/gating (figurative). Replace with plain words.

### 13. Inflated significance
"marks a pivotal moment", "stands as a testament", "underscores its
importance", "continues to thrive despite challenges", "the future looks
bright", stock "Challenges and Future Outlook" sections, send-off
paragraphs. Keep the fact, drop the significance, end on the last concrete
fact.
**Before:** "The institute was established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment
in the evolution of regional statistics."
**After:** "The institute was established in 1989."

### 14. Vague connection or association
"associated with", "in connection with", "linked to". State the relationship
the source gives. If the source does not say, keep the vague wording rather
than inventing a role.

### 15. Shallow -ing riders
"symbolizing", "reflecting", "underscoring", "ensuring", "showcasing" bolted
onto a simple fact. Keep the fact; keep the rider only when the source
supports it.

### 16. Sales language
"nestled", "breathtaking", "stunning", "must-visit", "rich heritage",
"renowned", "groundbreaking", "diverse array", "commitment to excellence".
State what the thing is.

### 17. Borrowed authority
"experts believe", "observers have cited", "industry reports", prestige
outlet lists. Name the real source and what it said, or cut the claim.

### 18. Avoiding is, are, and has
"serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "boasts", "features", "offers".
**Before:** "The gallery features four spaces and boasts 3,000 square feet."
**After:** "The gallery has four rooms totaling 3,000 square feet."

## D. Formatting by rule

### 19. Bold as decoration
Bold on every key term, labeled lists ("- **Performance:** Performance has
improved..."). Remove the bold; turn labeled lists into prose when the
labels add nothing.

### 20. Decorative headings
Title Case On Every Word, emojis and arrows in headings and lists, a
horizontal rule between every section, a document-opening heading that
repeats the document title. Use sentence case; cut decoration; name what the
section holds.

### 21. Curly quotation marks *(weak alone)*
Straighten “...” to "..." unless the required format says otherwise.

## E. Leftovers from the chat and the draft

### 22. Chatbot residue
"Great question!", "I hope this helps!", "Certainly!", "Let me know if...",
"Here is an overview of...". Remove the wrapper, keep the content.

### 23. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses
"as of [date]", "based on available information", "not widely documented",
"it is believed that", "she likely grew up...". State what the source shows
or cut the sentence. Never keep a disclaimer-wrapped guess.

### 24. A heading repeated in the first sentence
"## Performance" followed by "Speed matters." Delete the repeated line; let
the heading do its job.

### 25. Writing about the document instead of its subject
"This function was added to replace...", "The table below compares...",
"compiled from...". Describe the subject. Mention previous versions only in
change logs and release notes.

## F. Writing for the wrong reader

### 26. Re-explaining what the reader knows
A reply that restates the problem, walks the diagnosis, and reaches the
decision in the last line. Lead with the decision; keep only the fact the
reader lacks. Diagnosis and proof belong in the attached document.

## When not to act

Leave a flagged phrase alone inside: a quotation, a title, a proper name, or
a passage discussing the phrase itself. Salutations and sign-offs predate
chatbots. Text written before November 2022 is not AI text. One tell alone
is rarely proof; several tells in one passage are.
